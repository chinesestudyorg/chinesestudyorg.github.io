Reading Zhuangzi: stories, speakers, and perspective

Begin with what happens in a story. Then ask who explains it, who listens, and how the explanation changes what you noticed.

By ChineseStudy3 minute read15 October 2026

Begin with one story in the Inner Chapters

The current ChineseStudy Zhuangzi contents list the seven Inner Chapters, from Wandering Freely to Responding to Emperors and Kings. This is a published selection, not all 33 chapters of the received text. The Zhuangzi introduction offers a starting context; the first exercise needs only one story.

Trace Cook Ding’s actions

Open Nurturing Life and locate Cook Ding’s account of cutting up an ox. First follow the description of his movements. Note the moments of ease, then the difficult place where he slows down and attends closely. Keep the sequence visible before describing the episode as a lesson.

Make two columns in your notebook: an action you can point to, and the interpretation you are considering. For example, slowing down is an observable event in the account; what it suggests about skilled practice remains a claim to support. The distinction helps you notice details that an attractive summary might leave out.

Separate the speakers and their claims

The cook describes his practice, and the ruler responds to what he hears. Record each voice separately. What does the cook say has changed over time? What does the listener take from the account? Do not automatically make every speaker’s sentence a statement of the whole book’s position.

Read the translation for the sequence, then use the Chinese to revisit one expression. Pinyin can help you say the words, but it does not decide an interpretation. Leave the translator’s wording and your own paraphrase on separate lines.

Try a story-to-claim note

Record four things: what happens, who offers an explanation, what the listener concludes, and a detail that complicates your first reading. Add a link to the chapter. Write a question you could answer by rereading the surrounding passage, rather than a general claim about how to live.

Compare a change of perspective

Next, choose a short episode in Wandering Freely. Ask how a change of scale or viewpoint affects a judgment in that episode. Compare this with the cook’s account on one specific point; do not assume the two stories deliver an identical message. Readers using modern Chinese explanations can follow the same exercise in 《庄子》.

Keep the chapter open beside your reading note.
