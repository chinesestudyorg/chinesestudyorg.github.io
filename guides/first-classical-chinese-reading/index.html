Getting started

Your first classical Chinese reading

Choose a few lines you can return to. Your first useful reading begins with noticing, then asking a question you can carry into the next reading.

By ChineseStudy4 minute read6 October 2026

Choose something small

Start with one short poem or a brief passage. A manageable page gives you room to reread, and rereading is where your questions become more precise. Choose a text that interests you: an image you want to picture, a sentence that puzzles you, or a familiar title you have never opened.

For this first session, set a narrow goal: describe what the passage seems to be doing. You do not need a complete interpretation. If you know modern Mandarin, keep that knowledge available, but treat your first guess about a word as a possibility to check.

Read it three times

  1. Look at the Chinese. Notice recurring characters, the shape of the lines, and the punctuation in the edition you are using. Mark one pattern without explaining it yet.
  2. Read a translation. Use it to identify a speaker, a scene, or a main claim. Keep track of anything the translation makes explicit that you could not yet locate in the Chinese.
  3. Return to the original. Match a few clear words or phrases. Leave the rest open. Ask which detail changed your impression on the second visit.

Keep a small reading note

A useful note separates what you can see from what you infer. “This character appears twice” is an observation. “The repetition sounds insistent” is an interpretation. Both belong in your notebook; keeping them distinct helps you revise your reading.

Save the title, edition or webpage, and passage location with the note. Write one question that points back to the text. “Why does the speaker shift here?” gives your next reading more direction than “What does this all mean?”

Try a 15 minute reading

Spend five minutes with the Chinese, five with the translation, and five writing three lines: one observation, one tentative interpretation, and one question. Revisit those lines tomorrow before looking up more commentary.

Choose your next step

When a passage holds your attention, stay with it. You might compare another translation, look up a recurring word, or read the passage beside the one that follows. Let the question in your note decide which step will help.

Try this method on the opening chapter of the Tao Te Ching. Keep the Chinese and the translation in view as you make your first note.
